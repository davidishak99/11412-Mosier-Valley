My read

Start with front-and-back homes, keep townhomes as the stretch

Option C (four "B" lots, two detached houses on each) gets you 8 doors, uses the deep lots you called wasteful, and keeps every lot a 1–4 unit property. That means ordinary residential or DSCR loans, no syndication, and you can build one lot first to learn before committing to the rest.

Option E (about 12 townhomes) is the best yield that still sells one unit at a time to ordinary buyers. It needs a builder partner and real site work. If you find that partner, it beats C.

I would not self-develop apartments on one acre with no track record, directly across from a new 300-unit complex with amenities you can't match. If apartments are the goal, assemble more land or sell to someone who does it for a living.

All of this depends on one fact I could not confirm from your files: what the parcel is zoned today. Look that up before anything else. It decides which options are a permit and which are a rezoning case.

Without building it yourself

Four other plays

Assemble with a neighbor

Your own note says the city will want more land for real multifamily. Adding the tract to the east or west gets you past 2 acres with 420 ft of frontage, which is where a 40–60 unit project and its lenders start to make sense. Costs you a conversation to find out.

Entitle and sell

Rezone and plat, then sell the lots or the approved site to a builder. You capture the value the paperwork creates and skip construction risk. Lowest capital of anything here; the price is that the builder keeps the construction profit.

Put the land in as equity

Partner with a townhome or small-multifamily builder: you contribute the land, they bring the construction loan and the experience. This answers "funding is a mystery" without a syndication. You give up control and a share of the upside.

Hold

A 300-unit neighbor tends to pull utilities, traffic and attention to a street. Waiting a few years while paying taxes is a legitimate option, especially if the neighbors' tracts may come up for sale.

Before you spend on design

Eight things to find out first

  1. Current zoning and future land use. Check the parcel on the City of Fort Worth zoning map. A rezoning that matches the future land use map is far easier than one that fights it.
  2. Pre-development conference. Ask Development Services about road dedication (Mosier Valley Rd may call for more than 25 ft), required street improvements, driveway spacing and sidewalks.
  3. Water and sewer. Where the mains are and whether they have capacity. The apartment project probably extended or upsized them, which would help you, but confirm it. Ask for the per-unit impact fees while you are there.
  4. Stormwater. At 1.01 acres you sit right on the one-acre line that triggers state construction stormwater permitting. Ask whether detention will be required; it takes land out of every plan.
  5. Airport overlay. DFW Airport is close. Check for noise-contour or overlay restrictions on new housing.
  6. Title and deed restrictions. Confirm nothing recorded limits use, and that no easements cross the tract. The boundary survey shows none, but it is not a title survey.
  7. Fire access. Any plan with buildings deep in the lot (C, D, E, F) needs the fire marshal's view on hose reach and fire lanes early.
  8. The neighborhood. Mosier Valley is a historic freedmen's community with engaged residents. Talk to the council district office and neighbors before filing a zoning case, not after.

One state-law wrinkle: since September 2025, Texas SB 840 requires large cities to allow multifamily and mixed use on land zoned for commercial, office, retail or warehouse use, with exceptions (including sites near airports and heavy industry). If the parcel is or becomes commercially zoned, that could open an apartment path, or change how the city views a commercial rezoning. Ask a land-use attorney how Fort Worth is applying it here.
